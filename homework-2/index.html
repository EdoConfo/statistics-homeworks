Homework 2

One message, two ciphers. The same text is encrypted with a toy elliptic-curve scheme built on the Part 1 code and with the Caesar cipher. The Caesar cipher is then broken from letter statistics alone, and the toy scheme is broken by searching its small key space.

01

Encrypting with the toy secp256k1 scheme

Part 1 turned a private key k into a public key Q = kG. To encrypt with that key pair we use EC-ElGamal, which needs nothing beyond the point addition and double-and-add of Part 1. The code of Part 1 (ecc.js) runs unchanged on the real secp256k1 parameters. The only toy element is the private key, drawn from a small range [1, 2B] instead of [1, n − 1] with n ≈ 2²⁵⁶.

The algorithm

  1. Key generation (receiver). Pick k at random in [1, 2B] and publish Q = kG.
  2. Encoding. A curve can only add points, so the message must become a point. The text is split into blocks of up to 30 bytes, each read as an integer m. Then x = 256·m + j for the first j = 0, 1, 2, … that makes x³ + 7 a square mod p, and y is its square root: M = (x, y). About half of all x work, so j is usually 0, 1 or 2.
  3. Encryption (sender, knows only Q). Pick a fresh random r in [1, n − 1] and send C₁ = rG, C₂ = M + rQ.
  4. Decryption (receiver). Compute S = kC₁, which equals rQ because k(rG) = r(kG), and recover M = C₂ − S. Dropping the last byte of x gives back m and the text.

What they have in common

            
Both ciphers work the same way: add a secret mask in a group, subtract it to decrypt.
              Caesar adds K in the group of integers mod 26. EC-ElGamal adds the point S = rQ in the group of curve points.
              In both, decryption is just the inverse operation, and without the key the ciphertext cannot be turned back.

          

          

            
Where they differ

            
The difference is in the mask. Caesar uses one fixed mask for every letter, so the ciphertext keeps the statistics of the plaintext.
              EC-ElGamal draws a new random mask for every block from a group of about 2²⁵⁶ elements, and the receiver
              can rebuild it from C₁ only with k. If r were fixed, EC-ElGamal would become a Caesar cipher on curve points:
              equal blocks would give equal ciphertexts.

Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

        
A shift moves every letter by the same amount, so the shape of the letter distribution survives encryption.
          With a corpus of the language we know the shape to expect. Recovering the key means finding the shift that lines the two up.

Effectiveness

            
    
              
  • The χ² attack needs about one sentence. Its success climbs quickly with the length and passes 95%
                at around 20 letters. Beyond that, failures are rare accidents.
    • 
              
  • Exhaustive search is free here. 26 keys are nothing to try. The hard part is recognising the right plaintext automatically,
                and that is exactly what a statistical score does.
    • 
              
  • More context, fewer letters. Bigram scoring uses the order of the letters as well as their counts and needs
                about half as many letters as χ². The word list also uses the spaces between words.
    • 
            

          

          

            
Limitations

            
    
              
  • Short texts. With a handful of letters the sample frequencies are mostly noise.
                For HELLO WORLD, χ² prefers the key that gives WTAAD LDGAS: three A's, two D's and a T fit English
                letter counts better than the real message. Bigrams and the word list get it right (try it above).
    • 
              
  • Unusual texts. The method assumes the plaintext follows the corpus. Lists, codes, names, a text without E or text in another
                language can move the minimum. The "most frequent letter" rule fails first, the word list fails on other languages, while χ² often survives.
    • 
              
  • Only the Caesar cipher. One shift for the whole text is what makes the 26-way search possible. A general substitution
                (26! keys) still yields to frequency analysis but needs a smarter search, and a polyalphabetic cipher such as Vigenère first needs the period.
    • 
              
  • The language must be known, and the corpus must be large enough for its frequencies to be stable:
                with about 35 000 letters, the frequency of E is known to within about ±0.35 percentage points.

06
Attacking the restricted key space

        
The attacker sees the public key Q and the ciphertext of section 01, and knows that k ≤ 2B.
          Finding k is a discrete logarithm in a small interval, and once k is known the message decrypts exactly as for the receiver.

        

          

            
Brute force

            
Walk G, 2G, 3G, …, one point addition per step, until the point equals Q.
              On average this takes 2B−1 additions and almost no memory.
              Each candidate is checked exactly: a match is the key, with no statistics and no doubt.

          

          

            
Baby-step giant-step

            
Write k = i·m + j with m = ⌈√2B⌉. Store the baby steps
              jG for j = 1, …, m in a table, then take giant steps
              Q − i·mG for i = 0, 1, … until one lands in the table.
              About 2·2B/2 additions and 2B/2 stored points: time traded for memory.

Why this fails against real ECC

          
    
            
  • The key space. secp256k1 keys are uniform in [1, n − 1] with n ≈ 2²⁵⁶.
              Brute force needs about 2²⁵⁵ additions. The table above extrapolates the measured speed.
    • 
            
  • No shortcut is known. The best known attack on a well-chosen curve is Pollard's rho, a generic method
              that needs about √(πn/4) ≈ 2¹²⁸ operations. At 10¹⁸ operations per second, about a billion
              computers each doing a billion operations per second, that is still around 10¹³ years.
    • 
            
  • The curve has no special weakness. The order n of secp256k1 is prime, so the logarithm cannot be split into small
              pieces (Pohlig–Hellman), and none of the special-curve attacks (MOV, anomalous curves) apply.
    • 
            
  • Nothing statistical to grab. With a fresh random r the ciphertext points look uniformly random
              (section 03), so there is no letter-frequency side door like the one that breaks Caesar.
    •
