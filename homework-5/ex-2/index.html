Numerical Computation of the Normal CDF

Φ(x) has no formula in elementary functions. Why that is, what the error function adds, and how to get Φ(x) to full double precision quickly.

01

The function to compute

Φ(x) = P(Z ≤ x) = ∫−∞x φ(t) dt, φ(t) = e−t²/2 / √(2π)

φ integrates to 1: squaring ∫₀^∞ e−x² dx gives a double integral that polar coordinates turn into π/4. By symmetry Φ(0) = 1/2 and Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x), and any N(μ, σ²) reduces to it: P(X ≤ x) = Φ((x − μ)/σ).

02

Why there is no elementary formula

Elementary functions: those built from x and constants with +, −, ×, ÷, roots, exp, log (and so the trigonometric functions and their inverses), composed finitely many times.

Liouville's theorem

Let f and g be rational functions, g not constant. If ∫ f(x) eg(x) dx is elementary, then it equals R(x) eg(x) for some rational function R with

f = R′ + R g′

Applied to φ

Here f = 1 and g = −x²/2, so an elementary antiderivative needs a rational R with

R′(x) − x R(x) = 1
  • R a polynomial of degree d: xR has degree d + 1 and R′ degree d − 1, so the left side has degree d + 1 ≥ 1 and cannot be the constant 1. R = 0 gives 0.
  • R with a pole of order k ≥ 1 at some point c: R′ has a pole of order k + 1 there and xR one of order at most k. Nothing cancels the pole of order k + 1, but the right side has no poles.

No R exists, so ∫ e−x²/2 dx, and with it Φ, is not elementary. It does have an antiderivative (every continuous function does), and it can be written as a convergent series, as in section 04.

03

The error function

erf(z) = (2/√π) ∫₀z e−u² du ⇒ Φ(x) = ½ (1 + erf(x/√2))

With t = √2 u, ∫₀x φ(t) dt = ½ erf(x/√2); add Φ(0) = ½.

The formula is exact: Liouville's theorem shows that no elementary expression exists, so a new function is given a name and its properties (odd, increasing, erf(∞) = 1, derivative 2e−z²/√π, series, asymptotics) are studied once and reused. Φ is then a closed form in terms of erf, as sin is a closed form for the ratio of two sides.

A name does not give numbers. To print erf(1.3) to 16 digits someone has to evaluate an integral, a series or an approximation, exactly as libraries do for exp and sin. The difference is that exp and sin are built into every language; erf is not in JavaScript's Math, so here it has to be written.

04

Numerical approaches

All start from Φ(x) = ½ + ∫₀x φ(t) dt or from erf.

1. Trapezoidal rule

∫₀x φ ≈ h [½φ(0) + φ(h) + … + φ((n−1)h) + ½φ(x)], h = x/n

Error of order h².

2. Simpson's rule

∫₀x φ ≈ (h/3) [φ(0) + 4φ(h) + 2φ(2h) + … + 4φ((n−1)h) + φ(x)]

Error of order h⁴.

3. Power series

Φ(x) = ½ + (1/√(2π)) ∑ₖ (−1)ᵏ x2k+1 / (2ᵏ (2k + 1) k!)

e−t²/2 expanded and integrated term by term. Converges for every x, but the terms alternate in sign and grow to about ex²/2 before shrinking: for large |x| the cancellation destroys every digit.

4. Series with positive terms

Φ(x) = ½ + φ(x) (x + x³/3 + x⁵/(3·5) + x⁷/(3·5·7) + …)

The same function with e−x²/2 taken out of the sum (differentiate both sides to check). All terms have the same sign: no cancellation.

5. Continued fraction for the tail

1 − Φ(x) = φ(x) / (x + 1/(x + 2/(x + 3/(x + …)))) (x > 0)

Laplace's fraction. Converges fast for large x (about 30 levels at x = 4), slowly near 0. It gives the tail probability directly, without computing 1 − Φ as a difference.

6. Rational approximation of erf

erf(z) ≈ 1 − (a₁t + a₂t² + a₃t³ + a₄t⁴ + a₅t⁵) e−z², t = 1/(1 + 0.3275911 z)

Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26, with fitted constants aᵢ. One exponential and a polynomial, absolute error below 1.5 × 10⁻⁷. Then Φ(x) = ½(1 + erf(x/√2)).

Final method. Series 4 for |x| < 2.5, continued fraction 5 for |x| ≥ 2.5; for x ≤ −2.5 the fraction gives Φ(x) = 1 − Φ(|x|) directly, so even Φ(−8) ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹⁶ keeps all its significant digits. At most about 70 terms, no tables, no step size to choose.

Simpson needs about a hundred evaluations of e−t²/2 per point for 10 correct digits, and still fails in the tail. The final method uses at most about 70 multiplications and one exponential, and reaches full precision. The A&S formula is the fastest, a handful of operations, and is the right choice when 7 digits of absolute accuracy are enough.
