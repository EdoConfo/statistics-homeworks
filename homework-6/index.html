Statistical Independence

A contingency table built from raw records, its marginal and conditional frequencies, the frequencies expected under independence and the chi-square statistic that compares the two.

01

Dataset and contingency table

For example, take a log of n = 500 intrusion attempts recorded by an intrusion detection system. Each record carries two categorical variables: X = the targeted service (Web, SSH, Email, Database) and F = the outcome of the attempt (Blocked, Detected, Undetected). The log is simulated.

function contingencyTable(records, xKey, fKey) {
  const rows = [], cols = [], cells = new Map();
  const index = (list, v) => list.includes(v)
    ? list.indexOf(v) : list.push(v) - 1;
  for (const r of records) {
    const key = index(rows, r[xKey]) + '|' + index(cols, r[fKey]);
    cells.set(key, (cells.get(key) ?? 0) + 1);
  }
  const counts = rows.map((_, i) =>
    cols.map((_, j) => cells.get(i + '|' + j) ?? 0));

  ...
}

Contingency table: counts nᵢⱼ

Each cell nᵢⱼ is the number of records with X = xᵢ and F = fⱼ. The last column holds the row totals nᵢ·, the last row the column totals n·ⱼ.

02

Marginal and conditional frequencies

Dividing by the grand total gives the joint relative frequencies fᵢⱼ = nᵢⱼ / n; their row and column sums are the marginal frequencies fᵢ· = nᵢ· / n of X and f·ⱼ = n·ⱼ / n of F.

Conditional frequencies

Dividing by a row total instead gives the distribution of F among the records with X = xᵢ, f(fⱼ | xᵢ) = nᵢⱼ / nᵢ·. Dividing by a column total gives the distribution of X among the records with F = fⱼ, f(xᵢ | fⱼ) = nᵢⱼ / n·ⱼ.

03

Expected frequencies under independence

If X and F were independent, the joint frequency would be the product of the marginals, fᵢⱼ = fᵢ· f·ⱼ. Multiplying by n gives the counts expected in each cell, which keep the same row and column totals as the observed table:

Eᵢⱼ = n · (nᵢ· / n) · (n·ⱼ / n) = nᵢ· n·ⱼ / n

04

Chi-square statistic and p-value

Pearson's statistic adds up the squared differences between observed and expected counts, each scaled by the expected count:

χ² = ∑ᵢ ∑ⱼ (nᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ

It is 0 when the table matches independence exactly and grows with the distance from it. Under independence, for large n, it follows a chi-square distribution with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom, where r and c are the numbers of categories of X and F. The p-value is the probability, under independence, of a value at least as large as the observed one: p = P(Y ≥ χ²) with Y chi-square with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom.

Contribution of each cell (nᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ

05

Independence in probability and in statistics

In probability

Two events A and B are independent when

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B)

or, if P(B) > 0, when P(A | B) = P(A): knowing that B happened does not change the probability of A. Two random variables X and Y are independent when this holds for every pair of their events; for discrete variables,

P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) P(Y = y) for all x, y

that is, the joint distribution is the product of the marginals, and every conditional distribution equals the marginal one.

In statistics

On observed data the probabilities are replaced by relative frequencies. X and F are statistically independent in a table when

fᵢⱼ = fᵢ· f·ⱼ, i.e. nᵢⱼ = nᵢ· n·ⱼ / n

for every cell. Equivalently, all the conditional distributions f(F | xᵢ) are the same and equal to the marginal f·ⱼ (the bars of section 02 would all look like “All”), and then χ² = 0.

A sample from independent variables almost never gives χ² = 0 exactly, because of random fluctuations. The chi-square test decides whether the observed deviation is larger than what chance would produce: with null hypothesis H₀: X and F are independent, H₀ is rejected at level α = 0.05 when p < 0.05.

06

Interpretation and conclusions

What the test says

  • The test only says whether the two variables are associated, not how strongly: χ² grows with n, so with many records even a small association gives a small p-value. The conditional frequencies show where and how large the differences are.
  • A p-value above 0.05 would not prove independence either: it only says that the data are compatible with it, since a small sample may miss a real association.

Association is not causality

Dependence means that the two variables vary together, not that one causes the other. Here it does not prove that a service is itself harder to defend. The association could come from a third variable acting on both, for example the type of attack: phishing is aimed at email and is hard to detect, so attack type would link the service and the outcome. It could also come from how the data were collected, such as sensors that cover some services better than others. The direction can also be the reverse of the one assumed: a service whose attacks often go undetected may attract more attacks of the same kind.

Establishing a cause requires a controlled experiment, in which only the suspected cause is changed, or an analysis that accounts for the possible confounders. The chi-square test alone only detects the association.
