Modes of Convergence and Bayesian Inference

How a sequence of random variables can converge, which kind of convergence the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem give and why √n keeps appearing; then conditional probability and Bayes' theorem as the rule that turns a prior and a likelihood into a posterior.

Exercise 1

Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

01

The four modes of convergence

Let X₁, X₂, … and X be random variables on a probability space (Ω, 𝓕, P). A random variable is a function of the outcome ω ∈ Ω, so “Xₙ tends to X” can mean different things: that the numbers Xₙ(ω) converge for (almost) every ω, that large gaps become unlikely, that the average squared gap vanishes, or only that the distributions get close.

Almost sure convergence Xₙ →a.s. X

P( { ω : Xₙ(ω) → X(ω) as n → ∞ } ) = 1

Take one outcome ω and follow the ordinary sequence of numbers X₁(ω), X₂(ω), …: it converges to X(ω), and this happens for every ω outside a set of probability 0. It is a statement about whole trajectories: from some (random) index on, the path stays as close to the limit as we want and never leaves again.

Convergence in probability Xₙ →P X

for every ε > 0: P( |Xₙ − X| > ε ) → 0

At each large n, taken on its own, a deviation larger than ε is unlikely. It says nothing about a single trajectory: the outcomes that deviate at time n can be different from those that deviate at time n + 1, so a given path may still have large deviations infinitely often, as long as they become rarer and rarer.

Mean-square (L²) convergence Xₙ →L² X

E[ (Xₙ − X)² ] → 0

The average squared distance goes to 0 (it needs E[Xₙ²] < ∞). It controls the size of the deviations, not only their probability: a rare but huge deviation keeps E[(Xₙ − X)²] large. When the limit is a constant θ, E[(Xₙ − θ)²] = Var(Xₙ) + (E[Xₙ] − θ)², so L² convergence means that both the variance and the bias go to 0.

Convergence in distribution Xₙ →d X

Fₙ(x) = P(Xₙ ≤ x) → F(x) = P(X ≤ x) at every x where F is continuous

Only the distribution functions are compared, never the values: Xₙ and X do not even need to be defined on the same space. Equivalently, E[g(Xₙ)] → E[g(X)] for every bounded continuous g. It is what is needed to compute approximate probabilities P(Xₙ ≤ x) ≈ F(x).

02

How the modes are related

Almost sure and mean-square convergence each imply convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution. None of the arrows can be reversed in general, and neither of the two strongest modes implies the other.

Why the arrows hold

  • L² ⇒ P. By Chebyshev's (Markov's) inequality, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ − X)²] / ε², and the right side goes to 0.
  • a.s. ⇒ P. If almost every path eventually stays within ε, the probability of being outside at time n goes to 0 (dominated convergence applied to 1{|Xₙ − X| > ε}).
  • P ⇒ d. If Xₙ is close to X with high probability, the two have nearly the same distribution function at every continuity point.
  • d to a constant ⇒ P. If the limit distribution is a point mass at c, then P(|Xₙ − c| > ε) ≤ Fₙ(c − ε) + 1 − Fₙ(c + ε) → 0 + 1 − 1 = 0.

03

Role in the LLN, the CLT and other results

Let X₁, X₂, … be independent and identically distributed with mean μ and variance σ², Sₙ = X₁ + … + Xₙ and X̄ₙ = Sₙ / n.

Weak LLN: in probability

X̄ₙ →P μ

The easiest proof goes through L²: E[(X̄ₙ − μ)²] = Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n → 0, and Chebyshev gives P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). For a large fixed sample, the sample mean is close to μ with high probability.

Strong LLN: almost surely

X̄ₙ →a.s. μ

Kolmogorov's version only needs E|X₁| < ∞. It is a statement about the single infinite sequence of observations that we actually see: its running mean converges to μ. This justifies reading a probability as a long-run frequency and using simulation averages (Monte Carlo) as estimates.

CLT: in distribution

Zₙ = √n (X̄ₙ − μ) / σ →d N(0, 1)

Only the distribution converges, and it cannot be more: Zₙ and Z₂ₙ share only half of their terms and have correlation 1/√2 for every n, so Zₙ never settles on a value. What it gives is P(Zₙ ≤ z) ≈ Φ(z).

The LLN and the CLT describe the same sum at two scales. Divided by n, the fluctuations of Sₙ vanish and the limit is the constant μ, reached in the strong modes (a.s., L², P). Divided by √n, the fluctuations stay of fixed size and the limit is a whole distribution, reached only in the weakest mode.

04

Why √n: additivity of variance and independence

The variance of a sum is the sum of all the variances and covariances:

Var(Sₙ) = ∑ᵢ Var(Xᵢ) + 2 ∑_{i<j} Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ)

When the Xᵢ are independent (being uncorrelated is already enough) every covariance is 0 and the variance is additive: Var(Sₙ) = nσ². The standard deviation, which is in the units of the data, is the square root: SD(Sₙ) = σ√n. The typical size of the fluctuations of a sum of n terms is √n, not n, because independent errors partly cancel. Dividing by nᵃ gives

Var(Sₙ / nᵃ) = σ² n^(1 − 2a)

√n is the only scaling that keeps the variance finite and positive, which is why it is the scaling of the CLT (after centring: (Sₙ − nμ)/√n). The same fact gives the rate of the LLN: X̄ₙ − μ is of order σ/√n.

The role of independence

Without independence the covariances stay in the sum. If every pair has correlation ρ,

Var(Sₙ) = nσ² + n(n − 1)ρσ², Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n + (1 − 1/n)ρσ² → ρσ²

With ρ > 0 the variance of the mean does not go to 0 and the LLN fails: new observations repeat part of what the previous ones said (for example, n systems running the same software tend to fail together), so the sample behaves like only about 1/ρ independent ones. The scaling √n is no longer the right one either.

Additivity also needs finite variances: for Cauchy observations X̄ₙ has the same Cauchy distribution as a single X₁ for every n, and neither the LLN nor the √n rate holds.

√n in statistical inference

  • Standard error. The precision of the sample mean is SE(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n, estimated by sₙ/√n.
  • Confidence intervals. By the CLT and Slutsky, X̄ₙ ± 1.96 · sₙ/√n covers μ with probability close to 95%.
  • Tests. The z and t statistics √n (X̄ₙ − μ₀) / sₙ compare the observed distance with its standard error.
  • Sample size. Precision grows only like √n: halving the width of an interval needs four times as many observations, one more decimal digit needs a hundred times as many.
  • √n-consistent estimators. Most regular estimators (sample means, moment estimators, maximum likelihood) satisfy √n (θ̂ₙ − θ) →d N(0, v): their error shrinks at the rate 1/√n.
  • Independence assumed. All these formulas use Var(Sₙ) = nσ²; with correlated data (time series, clustered samples) the standard errors computed this way are too small.

Exercise 2

Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem and Statistical Inference

05

Conditional probability

For two events with P(B) > 0, the probability of A given B is

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Knowing that B happened shrinks the sample space to B; the probabilities inside it are rescaled so that they add up to 1 again. Two rules follow directly:

Product rule

P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)

Law of total probability (H₁, …, Hₖ a partition of Ω)

P(B) = ∑ⱼ P(B | Hⱼ) P(Hⱼ)

In probability theory

Conditioning is how information enters a model. It defines conditional distributions and the conditional expectation E[X | Y], it expresses independence (P(A | B) = P(A)), and it builds joint models step by step, as in Markov chains, where the next state depends only on the current one. In general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A): confusing the two is a common mistake, and Bayes' theorem is the rule that links them.

In statistics

Data x are modelled by a distribution f(x | θ) that depends on an unknown parameter θ. Frequentist inference treats θ as fixed and reasons with probabilities conditional on it: a p-value is P(T ≥ t | H₀), a confidence level is a probability over repeated samples for a given θ. Bayesian inference treats θ as a random variable and conditions the other way round, on the observed data: P(θ | x). The two answers are different questions.

06

Bayes' theorem: prior, likelihood and posterior

Writing the product rule both ways and dividing by P(B), then expanding P(B) with the law of total probability:

P(Hᵢ | D) = P(D | Hᵢ) P(Hᵢ) / ∑ⱼ P(D | Hⱼ) P(Hⱼ)

For a continuous parameter θ with density π(θ) and data x:

π(θ | x) = f(x | θ) π(θ) / ∫ f(x | θ′) π(θ′) dθ′ ∝ likelihood × prior

Prior π(θ)

What is known or assumed about θ before seeing the data: earlier studies, expert knowledge, physical constraints, or a deliberately vague distribution when little is known. It is a probability distribution over the possible values of θ.

Likelihood L(θ) = f(x | θ)

The model of the data read the other way: x is fixed at what was observed and θ varies. It measures how well each value of θ explains the data. It is not a distribution over θ and need not integrate to 1; only its shape matters, since constant factors cancel.

Posterior π(θ | x)

The distribution of θ after the data: the prior reweighted by the likelihood and normalized by the evidence ∫ f(x | θ) π(θ) dθ. Values that are both plausible a priori and good at explaining the data get high posterior probability.

How the theorem produces the posterior

  1. Choose the prior π(θ) and the model f(x | θ).
  2. Observe x and form the likelihood L(θ) = f(x | θ).
  3. Multiply pointwise: L(θ) π(θ) is the joint probability of θ and the observed data.
  4. Divide by its total, the evidence, so that the result is a distribution again.

In odds form, for two hypotheses: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio, P(H₁|D)/P(H₀|D) = [P(H₁)/P(H₀)] · [P(D|H₁)/P(D|H₀)].

Why updating is central to Bayesian inference

  • The posterior is the whole answer. Point estimates (posterior mean, mode), credible intervals with P(θ ∈ [l, u] | x) = 0.95, probabilities of hypotheses and predictions of new data are all read from it.
  • Learning is sequential. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior: updating with x₁ and then with x₂ gives the same result as updating once with both (for conditionally independent data), so evidence accumulates coherently.
  • Data eventually dominate. As n grows the likelihood becomes sharper while the prior stays the same; the posterior concentrates around the true value with a spread of order 1/√n (Bernstein–von Mises), whatever the prior, as long as the prior does not exclude the true value.
07

Example: is an IDS alarm a real attack?

For example, take an intrusion detection system that raises an alarm on 99% of attacks and, by mistake, on 5% of normal events. The hypotheses are attack and no attack; the prior is the base rate, the share of events that are attacks; the likelihoods are P(alarm | attack), the sensitivity, and P(alarm | no attack), the false-positive rate.

P(attack | alarm) = P(alarm | attack) P(attack) / [ P(alarm | attack) P(attack) + P(alarm | no attack) P(no attack) ]

Example: estimating a proportion with a Beta prior

For example, a company sends a simulated phishing e-mail to n employees and k of them click the link. The unknown parameter is θ, the probability that an employee clicks.

Prior

θ ~ Beta(a, b)

density ∝ θ^(a−1) (1 − θ)^(b−1), mean a/(a + b)

Likelihood

L(θ) = C(n, k) θᵏ (1 − θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ

binomial model, maximized at θ̂ = k/n

Posterior

θ | k ~ Beta(a + k, b + n − k)

same family as the prior (conjugate prior)

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed proportion:

E[θ | k] = w · a/(a + b) + (1 − w) · k/n, w = (a + b)/(a + b + n)

so the prior counts as a + b earlier observations, a clicks and b non-clicks.

The prior's weight w = (a + b)/(a + b + n) shrinks as n grows, so the three posterior means move towards k/n and towards each other; the strongest prior is the last to get there.

Updating one e-mail at a time

The same conclusion reached sequentially. The e-mails arrive one by one, each clicked with a true probability θ equal to the observed proportion above; after each one, each analyst's posterior becomes their prior for the next e-mail, so that after t e-mails it is Beta(a + kₜ, b + t − kₜ).

09

Applications, advantages, limitations and influence on conclusions

Applications

  • Diagnostic decisions with a known base rate: alarms, medical tests, fraud and spam filters (naive Bayes is this update with many features).
  • Estimating rates from few data, borrowing strength from earlier campaigns, similar systems or experts.
  • Sequential monitoring, where the estimate is updated as each new observation arrives.

Advantages

  • Prior knowledge enters explicitly and transparently instead of being ignored.
  • Direct answers: “θ is in [l, u] with probability 95%”, “P(attack | alarm) = 17%”.
  • Works with small samples, where a flat estimate such as k/n = 0/5 would be extreme.
  • Coherent sequential updating; uncertainty about θ carries over to predictions.

Limitations

  • The prior is a choice, and different analysts can reach different conclusions from the same data.
  • A prior that is confident and wrong needs many data to be corrected; a prior that gives probability 0 to a value can never be corrected.
  • The likelihood depends on the model: if f(x | θ) is wrong (e.g. alarms that are not independent), the posterior is wrong too.
  • Outside conjugate cases the evidence integral has no closed form and needs numerical methods such as MCMC.

Influence on the conclusions

To check how much a conclusion depends on the prior, the analysis can be repeated with several reasonable priors, as in the table of section 08: when they agree, the data speak for themselves; when they disagree, more data are needed or the choice of prior has to be justified.
