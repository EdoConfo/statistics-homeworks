Penetration Scores as Random Walks

m attackers each work through n systems. Every attempt adds a score Xᵢ, so the running total of each attacker is a random walk. Simulating many of these walks shows how the total, the average and the normalized total behave as n grows: the Law of Large Numbers, how the variance scales, and the Central Limit Theorem.

01

The model

Attacker j works through systems 1, 2, …, n and gets a score Xᵢ(j) at system i. All scores are independent and identically distributed. The attacker's total after k systems, Sₖ = X₁ + … + Xₖ, is a random walk: one trajectory per attacker, so m attackers give m independent trajectories.

Rademacher−1 or +1, ½ each01 +1: the attacker gains ground, −1: it is pushed back. Sₙ is the net progress, with no drift towards either side.

Independence makes the variances add up: Var Sₙ = Var X₁ + … + Var Xₙ = nσ². Dividing by c divides the variance by c², that is by n² for the average and by n for the normalized score. Everything else on this page follows from these three lines.

Why two models

The Bernoulli score only grows: Sₙ has a drift np that dominates, and the three quantities mostly differ in how they scale that drift. The Rademacher model is the symmetric, zero-mean case: there is no drift, only the fluctuations around 0, and these are what the Central Limit Theorem describes.

Total score

Each line counts the systems an attacker has penetrated so far (Bernoulli) or its net progress (Rademacher). The lines fan out like √k: after k systems two attackers typically differ by a few σ√k. In the Bernoulli model they also climb together along kp: more systems, more penetrations, for every attacker.

Average score

Each line is an attacker's success rate so far. After a few systems the rates are scattered over the whole range, after many systems every attacker is close to the same rate μ, inside μ ± 2σ/√k: a long campaign reveals the per-system success probability. This is the Law of Large Numbers, attacker by attacker.

Normalized total score

Dividing by √k exactly cancels the growth of the spread: the band √k·μ ± 2σ has the same width for every k. In the Rademacher model the lines stay centred on 0 and the histogram on the right is the normal bell of the Central Limit Theorem. In the Bernoulli model they drift upwards with √k·p.

03

Distributions as n grows

Sections 03 to 06 use one larger simulation: m attackers in each model, each followed for 10 000 systems, with the score recorded at n = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, …, 10 000. The same trajectories are read at increasing n.

How large should m be? Every histogram, mean and variance below is estimated from the m attackers, and the estimation error shrinks like 1/√m. With m = 5 000 a variance is estimated to within about ±4% (2·√(2/m)), enough to see each shape clearly: m = 1 000 is visibly noisier, m = 20 000 smoother but slower.

04

The average score and the Law of Large Numbers

The weak Law of Large Numbers says that Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ: for every tolerance ε > 0, P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞. With the variance σ²/n, Chebyshev's inequality proves it in one line:

P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Sₙ/n) / ε² = σ² / (nε²) → 0

What the chart shows

  • The probability goes to zero. The share of attackers whose average is off by at least ε falls to 0 as n grows, for every ε: a smaller tolerance only needs more systems. This is convergence in probability. For small n the curve zigzags because Sₙ/n can only take a few values; the trend is what matters.
  • Chebyshev's bound goes to zero too, and is what proves the law. It only uses the variance, so it sits well above the simulated share.

Reading it for attackers

Every attacker has the same success rate p per system. After a few systems the observed rates are scattered, after thousands of systems they are all close to p: a long attack campaign reveals the true rate, and a defender who watches many attempts can estimate p from the average. The precision improves only like 1/√n: one more decimal digit needs a hundred times more systems.

a = ½ is the only exponent that keeps the variance finite and non-zero. A smaller power lets the spread blow up, a larger one squeezes everything into a point. This is why √n is the scale on which a limit distribution can appear, while n is the scale of the Law of Large Numbers, where the limit is a constant.

The dots wobble slightly around the lines because each variance is estimated from m attackers (relative error about √(2/m)), so the fitted slopes are close to, not exactly, 1, −1 and 0.

06

The normalized score and the Central Limit Theorem

In the Rademacher model μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the Central Limit Theorem applies to Sₙ/√n as it is: Sₙ/√n converges in distribution to N(0, 1), that is P(Sₙ/√n ≤ x) → Φ(x) for every x, where Φ is the standard normal distribution function.

Histogram of Sₙ/√n and the N(0, 1) density

Distribution function of Sₙ/√n and Φ

What the charts show

  • A two-point start. S₁ is −1 or +1, nothing like a bell. Every system adds one more coin and the histogram fills in.
  • The bell appears early. By n = 20 the histogram already follows the N(0, 1) density, its mean and variance are close to 0 and 1, and about 95% of the attackers have |Sₙ/√n| ≤ 1.96.
  • A staircase that becomes Φ. Sₙ/√n only takes values 2/√n apart, so its distribution function is a staircase. The steps shrink as n grows and the staircase closes in on Φ: the largest gap, shown above the chart, gets smaller.

Why the Rademacher case

In the Bernoulli model Sₙ/√n has mean √n·p, which grows without bound, and variance p(1 − p): it does not converge to N(0, 1). It must first be centred and scaled, (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1 − p)) → N(0, 1). The symmetric, zero-mean Rademacher model is the case where Sₙ/√n converges as it is.

For the attackers: after n systems the net progress of a typical attacker is of order √n, and the share of attackers beyond c·√n in either direction is read off the normal table, about 5% for c = 1.96.

07

n systems, m attackers and the aggregate score

The aggregate penetration score of one experiment is the sum over all attackers, Tₙ = Sₙ⁽¹⁾ + … + Sₙ⁽ᵐ⁾, the sum of the whole table of section 01. In the Bernoulli model it counts every successful attempt of every attacker. It is a sum of nm independent scores, so the results above apply to it with nm in place of n:

E[Tₙ] = nmμ, Var Tₙ = nmσ², Tₙ/(nm) → μ in probability, Var(Tₙ/√(nm)) = σ²
Model

The aggregate trajectory: rate Tₖ/(km) of one experiment on 1 000 systems

How n and m change the aggregate score

What the system size n changes

  • Each attacker's own score. Its total has mean nμ and spread σ√n, its rate settles on μ (sections 02 to 04).
  • The aggregate score grows like nmμ, with a spread that grows only like σ√(nm).

What the number of attackers m changes

  • Not the distribution of a single attacker. The trajectories are independent and identical for any m. More attackers only give more trajectories, so the histograms and averages of the previous sections become more precise.
  • The aggregate score. m multiplies its expected value, E[Tₙ] = nmμ, while its spread grows only like √m: in the chart above, more attackers make the aggregate rate settle on μ sooner.

Only the product nm counts

For the aggregate score, systems and attackers are interchangeable: 10 attackers on 100 systems give the same distribution as 100 attackers on 10 systems, and the panels with the same nm have the same width. The relative spread, σ/(μ√(nm)), shrinks as the attack grows: for a defender, the total damage of a large attack is predictable. For example, with p = 0.3, 100 attackers on 100 systems penetrate 3 000 ± 92 attacker–system pairs (95% band), a rate of 30% ± 0.9%.

In the Rademacher model the aggregate net progress is centred on 0 with spread √(nm), and Tₙ/√(nm) converges to N(0, 1): attackers pushed back and attackers gaining ground cancel out on average.

  • The normalization chooses the question. Sₙ answers "how many systems", Sₙ/n "at what rate", Sₙ/√n "how large are the fluctuations around the trend".
  • Size brings predictability. The share of systems an attacker penetrates converges to p, and its uncertainty falls like 1/√n. The number of systems, on the other hand, keeps a spread of order √n.
  • More attackers do not change the law of one attacker, but they add up: the aggregate score has mean nmμ and spread σ√(nm), so only the product nm matters and the aggregate rate settles on μ faster as the attack grows.
  • The symmetric case is the cleanest setting for the Central Limit Theorem: with zero mean and unit variance, Sₙ/√n itself converges to N(0, 1).
  • The choice of m sets the precision of everything estimated from the simulation, with errors shrinking like 1/√m.
